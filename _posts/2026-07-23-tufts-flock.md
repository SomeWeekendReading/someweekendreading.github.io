## Automated Surveillance of Car Traffic  

There are these things called "automated license plate readers" (ALPRs).  They're
basically a camera, a computer, and a network interface that picks out and reports car
license plate numbers, along with date/time/location stamps.  They then report this data
to various authorities, usually either police or some large institution that owns them
(see below for an example of the latter).  The license plate data then gets looked up in a
state database to find the owners of the car, and with slight effort also in insurance
databases to find the licenses of the associated drivers to get individual names.  

_Key point:_  They do this _all the time,_ without any discrimination whatsoever about
which cars they grab.  Pure unwarranted search (though court cases have apparently found
loopholes for cops to use).  

The key questions for me are:  
- Why are there so many of them?  
- Where are they placed? 
  - Are we using them in a racist way to surveil minority or poor neighborhoods?  
  - Are we using them to make virtual gated communities around wealthy neighborhoods?  
  - Are they being used for surveillance of neighborhoods whose politics oppose those in power?  
- Are they placed permanently, or temporarily?  When contracts with surveillance companies
  are ended, are they ever actually removed?  
- Who gets to see the results, for how long, and for what purposes?  
- Why can't we have a no match/no memory rule, where any plate associated with a person
  for whom there's no current arrest warrant or not in hot pursuit is ignored? 
  - And can we get camera operators audited to enforce conforming with such a rule?  

Now we can answer some of those questions, and the answers are _not_ pretty:  

<a href="https://mefi.social/@MissConstrue/116927808934326955"><img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-07-23-tufts-flock-mastodon-1.jpg" width="550" height="863" alt="MissConstrue @ Mastodon: Flock cameras used for political surveillance" title="MissConstrue @ Mastodon: Flock cameras used for political surveillance" style="margin: 3px 3px 3px 3px; border: 1px solid #000000; margin: 0 auto; display: block;"></a>

As reported here by an acquaintance on social media, there are ways to track such
cameras (more ways below).  Some are via crowdsourcing, some are via requiring disclosure
from local governments (usually reluctantly with much foot-dragging), and some are just
from objective tracing WiFi and cellular signals.  

MissConstrue reports 2 quite interesting facts:  
1. There are a _lot_ of these involuntary surveillance cameras: around 115,000 at the time
   she looked, and 119,104 reported by Deflock when I just ran it this morning
   (2026-Jul-23).  (This is presumably within the United States, possibly the continental
   US, but I haven't checked.)  
1. The placement is _not_ random!  In her case, people in her neighborhood successfully
   got their town to defund the Flock cameras.  Some were removed, but all the ones
   pointed into her neighborhood of people who resisted surveillance were kept!  This is
   brazen, warrantless surveillance for _political_ purposes.  

It appears our fears above of racist or political motivation of surveillance were
correct.

Just _finding_ the cameras is a start, but many communities want to go further.  As

really, really _angry_ out there:  

> Backlash against the $7.5 billion startup Flock Safety—the maker of AI-powered, license
> plate-scanning, pole-mounted surveillance cameras—has gone from brewing to boiling over in
> recent weeks, much to the chagrin of the police state and its Silicon Valley boosters.   

There is significant legal and political pushback, trying to force local officials and
police who agreed to Flock agreements to terminate them.  Somewhat harder has been to get
the cameras actually removed, even if the agreements are terminated.  

This has led to a number of vigilante actions, involving spray-paint, axes, and
disassembly to steal & sell the copper and gold from the contacts inside the cameras
(apparently 2g Au + 1kg Cu, worth about \\$300?). Though lawless, this will no doubt
continue until the political system starts responding to people instead of money.  

(Note the phrasing of the headline in the article above: "take down" can be understood as
lawful efforts to get politicians and police to terminate contracts and remove cameras,
but "take down" _could_ also mean vandalism.  One should, of course, prefer the former.)  


## Hunting Flocks of Surveillance ALPRs in Deepest, Darkest Boston Suburbia  

So, of course, I couldn't resist trying it out.  The `maps.deflock.org` site didn't work
for me in Firefox, though admittedly I was reluctant to grant it as much Javascript and
remove as many blockers as it wanted.  It also didn't work in Safari, but did work in a
(regrettably permissive) Chrome installation.  

My particular suburban Boston neighborhood was not too bad, with cameras only at some
major intersections where accidents apparently happen.  I _hate_ the surveillance aspect,
but documenting car accidents _might_ be ok, if the data were publicly available.  

<a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-07-23-tufts-flock-deflock-tufts.jpg"><img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-07-23-tufts-flock-deflock-tufts-thumb.jpg" width="400" height="309" alt="Deflock results around Tufts University in Massachusetts, 2026-Jul-23, showing 28 Tufts-owned ALPR cameras on all approaches to campus" title="Deflock results around Tufts University in Massachusetts, 2026-Jul-23, showing 28 Tufts-owned ALPR cameras on all approaches to campus" style="float: right; margin: 3px 3px 3px 3px; border: 1px solid #000000;"></a>
Things got more ominous when I zoomed out.  A broader picture of neighboring towns showed
relatively sparse usage, with one exception that _really_ stands out against the background:
[Tufts University in Medford, Massachusetts](https://maps.deflock.org/?lat=42.4067&lng=-71.1192&zoom=15.10).
(__NB:__ If you use that URL, you will be getting updated info that might have changed
from the time I looked.)  

There are a minimum of 28 license plate surveillance cameras surrounding Tufts, apparently
guarding every approach to campus.  Inspection of each one shows that they are owned and
operated by Tufts itself, as shown in this screen capture taken on the morning of 2026-Jul-23.  

Tufts has decided to surveil _itself._  

They _probably_ are on Tufts-owned private property, so it's _probably_ legal (though I
don't know what Massachusetts state law says about privately-owned surveillance cameras).  

Why would Tufts do that? 
- Do they think there is a wave of crime, perpetrated by criminals dumb enough to drive to
  a crime scene in their own cars?  
- Do they want to track students?  
- Do they want to track faculty &amp; staff, particularly their work hours?  
- Who in the Tufts administration or police force has access to this surveillance data,
  for how long, and for what purposes?
- How &ndash; if ever &ndash; are they audited to ensure privacy policy compliance,
  whatever that privacy policy might be?  

I'm willing to bet that the answer to all those questions is, for now, "Pretty much nobody knows, and
those who do know aren't telling."

## Addendum 2026-Jul-30: The Venerable Globe Weighs In on Massachusetts Backlash Against Flock  

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-07-23-tufts-flock-globe-1.jpg" width="400" height="478" alt="Toby @ Globe: Massachusetts backlash against Flock surveillance cameras" title="Toby @ Globe: Massachusetts backlash against Flock surveillance cameras" style="float: right; margin: 3px 3px 3px 3px; border: 1px solid #000000;">
Just today, the venerable _Globe_ weighed in <sup id="fn2a">[[2]](#fn2)</sup> on the
apparent backlash across Massachusetts against Flock surveillance cameras and ALPRs for
tracking innocent people.  

It's one thing to acknowledge that approximately everybody has a phone with a camera.
People who think you're committing a crime or who witness police violence may record this
and publish the result.  That's at least under individual control.  

On the other hand, we're &mdash; regrettably &mdash; inured to some kinds of surveillance
cameras, such as Ring doorbells.  We get even less comfortable when we find that the data,
stored in the cloud, is used by police to surveil our neighborhoods.  The fact that this
is all done with no warrants whatsoever is even more galling.  

Yes, they help solve crimes.  Yes, they are also blatantly unconstitutional searches.
Given power and the secrecy with which to use it, it is _inevitably_ abused and lied about
to cover up that abuse(__emphasis__ added):  

> At the same time, some uses by law enforcement have been more controversial: Authorities
> in Texas, which has a near total ban on abortions, allegedly accessed Flock data to
> __investigate a woman suspected of having an abortion__, claiming they were working on a
> missing person’s case; some jurisdictions have __shared people’s data with Immigration and
> Customs Enforcement, or ICE__.  
>  
> Another concern privacy advocates raise is the lax oversight over searches in the
> system. To conduct a search, officers must select a reason for the search and have a
> case number. But the American Civil Liberties Union says it found __officers often just
> put down a single word: “investigation.”__ Earlier this year, two officers in Milwaukee
> used the technology to __search for their ex-girlfriends__ — one reportedly did so more than
> 120 times.  

So police, tending to the far right politically, tend to abuse these systems in ways that
are driven by their far right politics: tracking people who might be seeking reproductive
health care, tracking ex-girlfriends, offering locations to ICE for their kidnappings,
and so on.  

Most galling to me is the show of complete contempt for the requirement of justification,
with the single word "investigation": they think there should be _no_ limits on what they
can do, and any requirements to the contrary are just going through the motions.  

<a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-07-23-tufts-flock-globe-2.jpg"><img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-07-23-tufts-flock-globe-2-thumb.jpg" width="400" height="377" alt="Toby @ Globe: What Flock captures about your car" title="Toby @ Globe: What Flock captures about your car" style="float: right; margin: 3px 3px 3px 3px; border: 1px solid #000000;"></a>
There are _no_ restrictions on what police departments can do with the data, and _no_
restrictions on how long they can keep it if they download it.  If you're an activist
against ICE or police violence, expect them to know every trip you make in your car, for
_years._  

The excuse-making is also nearly endless:  

> Some of the opposition to Flock has its origins in the Trump administration’s
> controversial campaign to step up deportations of immigrants. __While Flock says it does not
> contract directly with ICE, the immigration agency has been able to access its data
> through cooperating law enforcement agencies that subscribe to Flock, according to
> published reports.__  

So Flock says it does not _itself_ share data with ICE, it is happy to supply data to
police who _do_ share data with ICE, with enthusiasm.  The link doesn't even have to be
direct: your police department might share data with another police department in, say,
Texas which has no hesitation at involving ICE.
