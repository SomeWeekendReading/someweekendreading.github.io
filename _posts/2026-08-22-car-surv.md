Remember when we wrote about the surveillance cameras tracking pretty much every car?
Remember when we wrote about the anti-surveillance fashion that makes ugly sweaters
diverting cameras?  Now imagine both at the same time.  


## Anti-Surveillance Wraps for Cars  

Here on this Crummy Little Blog That Nobody Reads (CLBTNR), we're not fans of fashion.
(It's a Nerd Tribe thing.)  But then again, we're _really_ not fans of warrantless dragnet
surveillance.  So we've previously written approvingly <sup id="fn1a">[[1]](#fn1)</sup>
<sup id="fn2a">[[2]](#fn2)</sup> of some 'fashion' designs that avoid surveillance using
what are called 'adversarial patterns' that confuse the AIs behind the cameras.  The
aesthetics are dubious, but we applaud the spirit of the thing.  

More recently, we've written on this CLBTNR somewhat dyspeptically on the automated
license plate readers (ALPRs) that seem to be everywhere, tracking every car without
warrant, building travel dossiers on more or less everybody. <sup id="fn3a">[[3]](#fn3)</sup>

At a recent [DEF CON (the annual computer security conference)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEF_CON),
[Bill Swearingen](https://www.linkedin.com/in/billswearingen) gave a talk on wrappers for
cars that amount to adversarial patterns to confuse the AI behind multiple different
surveillance cameras, including Flock. <sup id="fn4a">[[4]](#fn4)</sup>  

Here's an example wrapped around a car that, he claims, makes it more or less invisible to
ALPRs though _quite_ conspicuous to human beings.  Any cop who knows about this stuff will
immediately wonder, "Why is that guy evading cameras?  Maybe I better follow him."  

So, yeah: like the anti-surveillance fashion, it's conspicuous.  Surveillance companies
like Flock would probably _love_ to get their hands on samples to train their AIs to look
through it.  If that turns out to be too difficult, they'll push _hard_ to have it made
illegal, as in "felony illegal".  (Whether that will happen depends on ejecting
fascists from the US government, and the jury &mdash; that is, the electorate &mdash; is
still out.) <sup id="fn5a">[[5]](#fn5)</sup>

Swearingen has similar patterns for clothing, but is keeping his "best" patterns secrete
so surveillance companies can't train on them.  That will put a kink in our ability to use
them!  

<a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-08-22-car-surv-wikipedia-USS_West_Mahomet_ID-3681.jpg"><img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/images/2026-08-22-car-surv-wikipedia-USS_West_Mahomet_ID-3681-thumb.jpg" width="400" height="528" alt="Wikipedia: Dazzle camouflage making it hard to figure out which way the WWI ship USS West Mahomet is pointed" title="Wikipedia: Dazzle camouflage making it hard to figure out which way the WWI ship USS West Mahomet is pointed" style="float: right; margin: 3px 3px 3px 3px; border: 1px solid #000000;"></a>
Honestly, the whole episode reminds me of the WWI "dazzle camouflage" <sup id="fn6a">[[6]](#fn6)</sup>
that made ships conspicuous in some ways, but hard to figure out range, bearing, and
heading.  That, of course, made them harder to target with the elementary torpedoes and
artillery of the day.  

The example shown here is the _USS West Mahomet_, a WWI ship.  It's&hellip; well, obvious
that there's _a ship there,_ but it's less obvious exactly where the bow is and where it
might go if the props were turning.  

In the end, the statistics in WWI were uninformative.  Dazzle camouflage _may_ have helped
a bit: you get shot at more often because you're conspicuous, but more of those shots miss
because the enemy can't get a good firing solution.  


## The Weekend Conclusion  

The dazzle camouflage resonance is as informative as it is unfortunate.  The original was
a response to hostile militaries trying to kill ships.  Camouflage is a race between
adversaries, to hide from people trying to harm you.  Now, alas, US law enforcement
frequently behaves like a violent occupying army.  Many citizens seek ways to
defend themselves against law enforcement.  

Can't say I blame them.
